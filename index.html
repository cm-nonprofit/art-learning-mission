The Problem

Food banks are asked to feed more people with less certainty

Most neighborhood pantries plan next week's orders by gut feeling. When the guess is too low, families are turned away. When it's too high, fresh food spoils on the shelf. Both mistakes waste money that pantries don't have.

Demand swings weekly

School breaks, paydays, weather, and seasonal work all shift how many households show up — sometimes by 40% from one week to the next. Static ordering can't keep up.

Good data sits unused

Nearly every pantry already logs what it hands out each week — on clipboards, sign-in sheets, and spreadsheets. That history is a forecast waiting to happen, but nobody has time to analyze it.

Waste on both ends

Over-ordering perishables means produce composted instead of eaten; under-ordering staples means empty carts for the last families in line. Better estimates fix both at once.

Our Story

From a math project to a mission

Art Learning Mission began as founder Jack Fang's independent math project: could a simple model, fed a few weeks of a food bank's own distribution data, predict how much food would be needed next week? It could — and the answer was too useful to leave in a notebook.

Step 1 — The question

A weekend spent with spreadsheets

Volunteering at a local pantry, Jack noticed the coordinator estimating next week's order from memory. He asked for the past three months of distribution logs and started charting them, looking for the patterns hiding in plain sight.

Step 2 — The model

Forecasting from twelve weeks of history

Using moving averages and weekly seasonality — math any volunteer can audit — the model predicted household turnout and food category demand from prior weeks' records. Backtesting showed it beat gut-feel ordering week after week.

Step 3 — The pilot

One pantry says yes

The pilot pantry began ordering from the forecast instead of the guess. Produce spoilage dropped within the first month, and the coordinator stopped running out of canned staples on Fridays. Other pantries started asking how it worked.

Step 4 — The mission

Art Learning Mission is born

To bring the tool to every pantry that wants it — free, transparent, and simple enough to run on the laptop already sitting in the back office — the project became a nonprofit with four core programs.

What We Do

Four programs, one goal: no more guesswork

Every program grows directly out of the original forecasting project — turning weekly distribution records into decisions pantries can trust.

Core Toolkit

ForecastForge

Our free forecasting toolkit. A pantry uploads (or types in) its last 8–12 weeks of distribution logs and gets back a plain-English estimate for next week: how many households to expect and how much of each food category to order. No jargon, no subscription, no data leaves the building.

Weekly demand forecasts
Dashboards

Shelf Arc

A visual inventory tracker that plots each food category's stock trajectory against its forecast. Coordinators see a shortfall coming five days out — not the morning it happens — and get simple reorder suggestions sized to the projected gap.

Shortfalls flagged days ahead
Training

Data Bloom Fellows

A hands-on training track for pantry staff and student volunteers. Fellows learn to clean sign-in sheets, chart weekly trends, and run the forecast themselves in a four-session workshop — so the math stays in the community, not with us.

4-session workshops
Network

Pantry Nexus

Pantries in the same region often see the same demand spikes a week apart. Nexus pools anonymized weekly counts across partner pantries so each site's forecast learns from its neighbors — and surplus produce at one site can be routed to a predicted shortfall at another.

Regional demand sharing

Why forecasting is a hunger intervention

A dollar a pantry doesn't waste is a dollar it spends on food. When ordering matches real demand, the same budget serves more families, fresher produce reaches plates instead of compost bins, and the last household in line finds the same shelves as the first. That's the multiplier we exist to unlock — not more donations, but more nutrition per donation.

The Founder

Meet Jack Fang

Jack Fang

Founder & Lead Forecaster · Prospective Mathematics Major · Student ID 1887

Jack is a student heading into a mathematics degree who believes the most useful math is the kind a volunteer can check with a pencil. His project began with a simple observation at a local food bank: the hardest part of the job wasn't lifting boxes — it was knowing how many boxes to order.

He built a forecasting model on the pantry's own weekly records, tested it against months of history, and watched it out-predict experienced guesswork. Art Learning Mission is his effort to hand that capability to every pantry coordinator who wants it, with training that makes the community self-sufficient rather than dependent.

"Every pantry already owns the data it needs. My job is just to teach the shelves to read it."
